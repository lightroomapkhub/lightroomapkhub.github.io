Masking Tutorial: Selective Edits in Lightroom Mobile

What Masking Does in Lightroom Mobile

Masking lets you edit one part of a photo without touching the rest. Instead of brightening the whole image to save a dark subject, you can brighten only the subject. Instead of darkening an entire landscape to deepen the sky, you can darken only the sky. A mask is simply a selection: the area inside the mask receives your adjustments, and everything outside it stays exactly as it was.

This is the single most powerful editing skill in Lightroom Mobile. Global adjustments are blunt tools, and they always involve a trade-off: fixing one area often damages another. Masks remove that trade-off. Once you can select a subject, a sky or a background reliably, you can make edits that look like they were done by a professional, because the light in each part of the photo is treated on its own terms.

Select Subject and Select Sky

The fastest masks are the automatic ones. Select Subject uses the app's detection to find the main person, animal or object in the frame and select it. Select Sky finds the sky, even when it is broken up by trees, buildings or mountains. Tap the masking icon, choose one of these options, and Lightroom builds the selection in a second or two.

Automatic masks are usually very good, but check the edges before you edit. Tap the overlay view to see the selection as a red tint, and look closely around hair, glasses, branches and rooflines. If the mask missed a small area, you can add to it with a brush; if it grabbed something it should not have, you can subtract with a brush. A minute spent refining the mask saves you from strange halos and glowing edges later, which are the classic signs of a rushed mask.

Select Sky is especially useful for landscapes. A dull grey sky can be darkened and given more blue, while the land below stays bright and natural. Try lowering the sky's exposure slightly, adding a little contrast, and cooling the white balance. The change is dramatic, but because only the sky is affected, the rest of the photo stays believable.

Background, Linear and Radial Masks

Select Background is the mirror image of Select Subject: it selects everything except the main subject. It is the right choice when you want the background darker, blurrier-looking or less colorful so the subject stands out. A common portrait move is to lower the background exposure by about half a stop and reduce its saturation slightly, which makes the person pop without any harsh cutting out.

Linear masks, also called gradients, cover an area that fades smoothly from full effect to no effect. Drag one from the top of the frame downward to darken a bright sky, or from the bottom upward to lift a dark foreground. Radial masks are oval selections that fade at the edges, perfect for brightening a face, drawing attention to a product, or adding a soft glow to one corner of the frame. Both can be rotated, resized and repositioned after you place them, so do not worry about getting the shape perfect on the first try.

Painting with the Brush Mask

The brush mask is the freehand option: you paint exactly where you want the effect. It is the best choice for irregular areas that no automatic tool can find, such as a single flower in a field, a patch of water, or one wall of a building. Choose a brush size that suits the area, set the feather high so the edges blend softly, and paint with overlapping strokes.

Feather is the setting that matters most. A hard-edged brush leaves a visible line where the edit starts and stops, while a well-feathered brush fades the effect so gradually that no one can see the boundary. Zoom in when painting near an edge you want to respect, such as the outline of a person, and use the erase mode to clean up any stroke that crossed the line. If your finger is too imprecise, a stylus helps, but most brush work is perfectly doable with a fingertip and a little patience.

Tip: Name your masks as you create them, such as "Sky" or "Subject face". On a photo with three or four masks, clear names stop you from adjusting the wrong one.

Combining Masks for a Finished Edit

Real photos usually need more than one mask. A typical portrait might use Select Subject to brighten the person, a radial mask to add warmth to the face, and a linear mask to darken a bright window behind them. A landscape might combine Select Sky with a brush mask on the foreground. You can stack as many masks as you like, and each one holds its own adjustments.

The key to combining masks is subtlety in each one. Three gentle masks look natural; three aggressive masks stacked on the same photo look over-processed. Finish your global adjustments first, then add masks one at a time, checking the whole image after each one. When you are done, toggle all masks off and on to compare with the original. If the masked version looks like a better version of the same photo rather than a different photo, you have used masking well.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a subscription to use masking?

Basic masking, including Select Subject and Select Sky, is available in the free version of Lightroom Mobile, while some advanced tools such as the healing brush and certain selective edits require a premium subscription. Check the current feature list in the app, as Adobe changes it from time to time.

Why does my mask leave a bright halo around the subject?

A halo usually means the mask edge is too hard or the adjustment is too strong. Increase the feather, reduce the strength of the adjustment, and refine the mask edge with a brush to remove the spill.

Can I copy a mask from one photo to another?

Yes. When you copy edit settings from one photo and paste them onto another, masks are included. They work best when the two photos have a similar composition, and you can reposition a linear or radial mask after pasting.
