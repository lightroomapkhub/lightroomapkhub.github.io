Landscape Editing Tutorial for Lightroom Mobile

Recover the Sky First

The sky is usually the brightest part of a landscape photo, and it is also the first thing that gets lost. Open your photo in Lightroom Mobile and pull the highlights slider down. In many photos this alone brings back clouds and colour that looked completely white. If the sky is still blown out, lower the exposure slightly and then lift the shadows to recover the foreground. For more control, use a linear gradient mask: drag it from the top of the frame down to the horizon, then reduce the exposure and highlights inside the mask only. This darkens the sky without touching the land below.

Balance the Light Across the Scene

Landscapes often have a huge difference between the brightest sky and the darkest foreground, more than a phone camera can capture in one shot. After recovering the sky, balance the rest of the scene. Lift the shadows to reveal detail in trees, rocks and the darker side of hills, but stop before the shadows turn grey and noisy. If the foreground is still too dark, add a second linear gradient from the bottom of the frame up, and raise the exposure and shadows inside it. Set the overall exposure so the middle of the scene looks right, then use the whites and blacks sliders to set the ends of the range: the brightest cloud should be near white, and the darkest shadow should be near black, but neither should be a flat block of colour. A little contrast helps the scene feel solid, but too much contrast crushes the shadows you just recovered. The goal is a photo where the eye can travel from the foreground to the horizon without hitting a wall of black or white.

Colour Without Overdoing It

Colour is where most landscape edits go wrong. The temptation is to push the saturation slider up until the grass glows and the sky burns blue, but the result looks like a postcard from a different planet. Use vibrance instead of saturation: vibrance strengthens the weaker colours first and protects skin tones and colours that are already strong. Then open the Colour Mix panel and work colour by colour. Deepen the blue of the sky and water with the blue luminance slider, and enrich green foliage with a small saturation increase in the green channel. For sunsets, warm the orange and yellow hues slightly and be careful with the red channel, which can quickly turn a sunset pink or purple. The finished photo should look like the place felt when you stood there, not like a filter applied on top of it.

Add Depth and Texture

A flat landscape photo usually lacks depth, and Lightroom has three sliders that fix this: Texture, Clarity and Dehaze. Texture brings out fine detail in rock, bark and grass without affecting the smooth areas such as sky and water. Clarity adds midtone contrast, which makes mountains and cliffs feel solid and three-dimensional, but too much clarity creates harsh halos along edges, so use it in small amounts. Dehaze cuts through mist and atmospheric haze, which is useful for distant mountains, but it also darkens the photo and adds contrast, so balance it with a small exposure lift. Use a radial mask to add depth in a specific place: brighten a lake reflection or a sunlit hillside slightly, and the eye will be drawn along the path you create. Foreground interest matters too. If there is a rock, a flower or a fence in the lower part of the frame, sharpen it a little with a brush so the photo has a clear place to start before the eye travels to the distance.

Tip: Shoot landscapes in RAW (DNG) when your phone supports it. A RAW file holds far more detail in the sky and shadows than a JPEG, so the highlight and shadow recovery in this tutorial works much better.

Finishing: Sharpening, Crop and Export

Finish with the small steps that make a landscape look complete. Straighten the horizon first, because even a one-degree tilt is obvious in a landscape with a lake or a sea. A tilted horizon is the most common mistake in otherwise good landscape photos. Check the crop: remove distracting objects at the edges, such as a branch poking in from the corner or a bright car at the edge of the frame, and keep the horizon out of the exact middle unless the reflection is the subject. Add sharpening in the Detail panel, but keep the amount modest and raise the masking slider so the smooth sky and water are not sharpened into noise. A light vignette can frame the scene, but skip it if the corners already hold important detail. Finally, compare the edited photo with the original by tapping and holding the image. If the edit makes the place look more like you remember it, it is done. Export at full quality for printing, and remember that a photo edited for a phone screen often needs slightly less contrast when viewed on a large monitor.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my sky white even after lowering the highlights?

The sky may be truly overexposed with no detail recorded, especially in JPEG photos. Shoot in RAW next time, and expose for the sky by tapping the brightest part of the scene before taking the photo.

Should I use Dehaze on every landscape?

No. Dehaze helps with distant haze and mist, but on a clear day it adds unwanted contrast and darkens the photo. Use it only when the scene actually looks hazy.

How much saturation is too much?

If the colours look brighter than the real place felt, it is too much. A good check is to step away from the phone for a minute and look again with fresh eyes.
