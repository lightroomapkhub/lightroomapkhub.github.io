Best Export Settings in Lightroom Mobile

Why Export Settings Matter

You can spend an hour perfecting an edit and still end up with a dull, soft or oddly colored photo if the export is wrong. Exporting is the step that turns your edited file into a finished image, and the settings you choose decide its file type, size, quality and color. The right settings depend entirely on where the photo is going: a file made for Instagram needs different treatment from one made for a large print, and a backup copy needs different treatment again.

Lightroom Mobile keeps your edits non-destructive, which means the original file is never changed and you can export the same photo many times with different settings. That freedom is worth using. Export a small, sharp copy for social media, a full-quality copy for your archive, and a print-ready copy when you need one. There is no single perfect export setting, only the right setting for each destination.

File Type: JPEG, PNG or Original

For almost every purpose, JPEG is the right choice. It is supported everywhere, produces reasonably small files, and at high quality settings the compression is invisible. When you export as JPEG, set the quality slider to 90 percent or higher. Below 80 percent you start to see blocky artifacts in smooth areas such as skies, and there is rarely a good reason to go that low when storage is cheap.

PNG is the better choice only when you need transparency or perfectly crisp edges, such as a logo or a graphic. For photographs, PNG files are much larger than JPEGs with no visible benefit, so avoid them for normal photos. The Original option exports the file in its original format, which is useful if you shot in RAW and want to keep working on the file in another app, but an original RAW export is not a finished photo and most apps and websites cannot display it properly.

Quality and Dimensions for Social Media

Social media apps compress every photo you upload, so the trick is to give them a file that survives that compression well. Export at full resolution with quality set to 90 to 100 percent, and let the app do its own resizing. Shrinking the photo yourself before uploading usually makes it softer, because it gets compressed twice.

For Instagram, a portrait post at 1080 pixels wide by 1350 pixels tall (a 4:5 ratio) uses the most screen space in the feed, while square posts work at 1080 by 1080. For stories and reels, use 1080 by 1920. If your photo is smaller than these sizes, do not upscale it in the export; enlarging a small image only makes it blurry, and it is better to post it at its natural size. Also check that your export keeps the crop you intended, since some apps re-crop portrait photos automatically.

Exporting for Print

Printing is where resolution matters most. A good rule is 300 pixels per inch: divide the pixel dimensions of your export by 300 to find the largest print size that will stay sharp. A photo that is 6000 by 4000 pixels, for example, prints well up to 20 by 13 inches. For a standard 8 by 10 inch print, you need at least 2400 by 3000 pixels, which most modern phone cameras produce easily.

Export print files as JPEG at maximum quality, or as TIFF if your print shop accepts it and you want zero compression. Set the color space to Adobe RGB or ProPhoto RGB only if your print shop asks for it; otherwise sRGB is the safer choice, because it is the standard that screens and most printers expect. If a print ever comes back looking darker than your screen, the cause is usually screen brightness: edit and judge prints with your screen at a moderate, consistent brightness rather than maximum.

Tip: Before exporting a batch of photos, export one test image and view it on the device or platform where it will be seen. A five-minute check catches color, sharpness and cropping problems before they affect the whole set.

Color Space, Sharpening and Backup

Color space is one of the most misunderstood export settings. sRGB is the safe default for anything viewed on a screen, including social media, websites and messaging apps, because every screen understands it. Wider color spaces such as Adobe RGB can hold richer colors, but if you export in Adobe RGB and view the photo on a device that expects sRGB, the colors can look washed out. Unless you have a specific reason and a calibrated workflow, choose sRGB.

Sharpening for output is a separate step from the sharpening in your edit. Lightroom Mobile applies a modest amount of output sharpening automatically on export, which is enough for most uses. Avoid adding heavy sharpening before export, since the combination of edit sharpening and export sharpening creates crunchy halos around edges. Finally, treat exports as disposable and originals as precious: keep your original files and your Lightroom catalog backed up, and re-export whenever you need a different version, instead of editing an already exported JPEG again.

Frequently Asked Questions

What quality setting should I use for JPEG exports?

Use 90 to 100 percent for anything you care about. The file is a little larger, but the difference in visible quality is real, especially in skies and smooth gradients.

Why do my Instagram photos look less sharp than the original?

Instagram recompresses every upload. Export at full resolution and high quality, avoid upscaling small images, and check that your crop matches Instagram's ratios so nothing is cut off.

Should I export in sRGB or Adobe RGB?

Choose sRGB unless a print shop specifically asks for Adobe RGB. sRGB is understood by every screen and app, while Adobe RGB files can look dull on devices that do not support it.
