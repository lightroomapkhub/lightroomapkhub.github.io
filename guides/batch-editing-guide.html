Batch Editing Guide: Edit Hundreds of Photos Fast

Why Batch Editing Matters

A wedding can leave you with eight hundred photos. A holiday can leave you with five hundred. Editing them one at a time is not just slow, it is inconsistent: the first photo gets your full attention, the fiftieth gets a tired guess, and the three hundredth gets whatever you remember from the first. Batch editing fixes both problems at once. You build one careful edit, then apply it to every photo shot in similar light, so a whole set looks like it belongs together and the work takes minutes instead of days. The trick is knowing which settings are safe to copy and which ones need a human eye on each frame. Get that balance right and batch editing becomes the single biggest time saver in your workflow.

Start With One Strong Edit

Batch editing begins with a single photo, not with a button. Pick a representative shot from the set, ideally one with average brightness and the skin tones or colours that matter most, and edit it properly. Set the white balance first, because colour temperature shapes everything that follows. Then fix exposure, lift the shadows a little, pull back any blown highlights, and add a touch of contrast and clarity. Finish with gentle colour work: a small vibrance boost and, if needed, a slight saturation change. Save this as your base look before you touch anything else. A rushed base edit multiplied across three hundred photos is three hundred mediocre photos, so take your time here. When the base photo looks right on your phone screen and on a larger display, it is ready to share.

Copy and Paste Settings

The simplest batch method in Lightroom mobile is copy and paste. Open your finished photo, tap the three-dot menu, and choose Copy Settings. You will see a list of every adjustment group: light, colour, effects, detail, optics and geometry. Tick the ones you want to carry over and leave the rest unchecked. Then open the next photo, open the same menu, and choose Paste Settings. The edit lands in a second. This method is ideal when you have a small run of similar shots, perhaps ten ceremony photos taken within a few minutes of each other. It is also the most controlled method, because you review each photo as you paste. The downside is repetition: pasting onto two hundred photos one by one is still slow, which is where syncing comes in.

Tip: When copying settings, uncheck Crop and Healing. A crop that fits a wide group shot will ruin a close portrait, and a healed spot copied from one photo can land on a face in the next.

Sync Edits Across Many Photos

For larger sets, select multiple photos and apply the edit to all of them at once. On Lightroom desktop, select your edited photo plus the targets, then use Sync Settings and choose exactly which adjustments to sync. On mobile, you can select several photos in the library and paste copied settings onto the whole selection in one step. Work in groups that share the same lighting: all the outdoor ceremony shots together, all the indoor reception shots together, and all the golden-hour portraits together. Never sync one edit across a whole wedding, because the light changes from the church to the garden to the hall, and a single look will be wrong in at least one of those places. After syncing, skim through the set quickly and fix the outliers. Most photos will need nothing; a few will need a small exposure nudge.

Presets and a Repeatable Workflow

Presets turn batch editing into a one-tap habit. Once your base edit is good, save it as a user preset with a clear name, such as Wedding Outdoor or Travel Sunset. The next time you shoot in similar conditions, apply the preset to the whole set, then adjust exposure per photo. Build a small library of five or six presets that cover your usual situations instead of collecting hundreds you never use. A reliable workflow looks like this: import and back up, delete the obvious failures, sort into lighting groups, edit one photo per group, sync or paste to the group, skim and fix outliers, then export. Keep the order the same every time. A repeatable process removes decisions, and removing decisions is what makes a five-hour job take one hour.

Common Batch Editing Mistakes

The most common mistake is syncing everything, including local adjustments. A gradient that darkens the sky in one photo may darken a face in another. Masking, healing and crops should almost always stay manual. The second mistake is trusting your phone screen in bright sunlight: an edit that looks fine outdoors can look dark indoors, so check your base edit in normal indoor light before syncing it. The third is over-editing the base photo. Heavy clarity, strong saturation and aggressive sharpening look dramatic once and harsh three hundred times. Batch edits should be gentle and clean; the drama belongs in a few hero shots you finish individually. Finally, do not skip the final skim. Batch editing is fast, but it is not automatic, and ten minutes of review is what separates a professional-looking set from a set that looks processed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I batch edit in the free version of Lightroom mobile?

Yes. Copy and paste settings works in the free version, so you can apply one edit to as many photos as you like without a subscription.

Will batch editing make all my photos look identical?

No, if you group by lighting and only sync global settings like exposure, white balance and colour. Each photo keeps its own composition, and small per-photo tweaks keep the set natural.

Should I sync crops and healing when batch editing?

Usually not. Crops, masks and healing are specific to one frame, so copying them often creates new problems. Sync the colour and light settings, and handle crops individually.
